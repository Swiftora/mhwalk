North Main Extension

Old Farmers National Bank

1903 · 28 North Main Street

Extension stop N2 of 5 · Old Town Hall Museum 9 min · Map

On October 11, 1932, four young men walked into this bank, tied up everyone inside, and drove off with 8,370 Depression dollars.

Two employees and two customers were in the banking room that morning. The robbers put them facedown on the floor and worked fast. From the garage opposite, the owner saw it happen, grabbed another man and a car, and chased the bandits out of town before losing them. In the fourth year of the Depression, in a village where everyone knew everyone, four strangers had emptied the farmers' own bank and vanished down the pike.

It was the farmers' own bank in the plainest sense. Its stockholders held their organizing meeting on March 21, 1903 in Old Town Hall, the building where this tour begins. That same year, in the flush of the produce boom, they put up this stone landmark on ground newly cut from the old mill property. A Flemish gable, a corner tower on a squat column, arched windows under an embossed metal cornice: the historic district survey reads the design as Romanesque Revival "showing the influence of Philadelphia architect Frank Furness" and calls it highly original. The farmers admired the vault.

The Depression that framed the robbery never broke the bank itself. Banks failed by the thousands in those years, and in March 1933 Washington ordered every bank in the country closed in the national bank holiday. The farmers' bank came through all of it whole, never failed and never in receivership, and twenty years on it was still greeting customers under the slogan it had earned: The Friendly Bank.

The bank outgrew the building in 1963 and moved up Main Street, and the township government moved in. For over a decade Harrison Township was run from a bank, complete with a celebration out front in 1972 when the town dedicated its new police car. By the mid-1970s the township needed more room too, and government moved on to Bridgeton Pike.

Did You Know

The robbers were never caught by the men who chased them. The 8,370 dollars they took in 1932 was serious money, worth close to two hundred thousand dollars today.
