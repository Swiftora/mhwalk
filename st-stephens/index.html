North Main Extension

St. Stephen's Episcopal Church

1853 · 51 North Main Street

Extension stop N4 of 5 · Old Town Hall Museum 12 min · Map

When this congregation built its new church, it had to bring its dead along. What the gravediggers found became a local sensation.

Episcopal worship in Mullica Hill began, by tradition, around 1800 in the gristmill down by the creek, with a layman named Joseph Chatham reading the services. A proper church followed in 1813 at South Main and East Church Streets, the township's second house of worship, at the far end of the village you have just walked.

Stone came at mid-century, and it came the hard way. The parish's own history records that the stone is not local at all: it was quarried in Pennsylvania, brought by barge to Mantua, and hauled here by cart. The diamond-paned windows are believed to be Glassboro work, and the iron panels of the chancel, which serve today as the altar rail, are credited by parish tradition to the village's own foundry. The cornerstone went down on April 1, 1852; the finished church was consecrated on August 30, 1853, at a cost so steep the parish had to sell its parsonage to clear the debt. Then the congregation moved its cemetery. Custom put the churchyard beside the church, so the graves at the old site were opened and the burials carried up Main Street to this ground. The gravediggers made a discovery the village never forgot: several of the dead, the Society's book records, were "in a perfect state of preservation." The old frame church, sold out of holy use, did time as a private school, ended as a piggery, and was finally pulled down.

The tower is a story of its own. John Schwebel built it in 1879, with a bell cast that year by Henry McShane of Baltimore, and the parish history records that the first hand to ring it, on September 11, 1879, belonged to Joseph A. Chatham, a descendant of the layman who had read those first services in the gristmill. The next year the whole town, of every denomination, subscribed the money that put the clock in the tower. That detail matters: the clock was the town's, not just the parish's. It has been the town clock ever since, and the little law office next door at Stop N5 has kept it company for well over a century.
